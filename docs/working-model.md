# Working model: Mexican street fruit cart in the United States

**Date:** August 2026
**Status:** research and operating plan
**First market:** San Antonio (see [first-market.md](first-market.md)). Houston is cart two. Austin is a later high-price site.

---

## 1. What we are copying

Mexican street fruit — *fruta picada*, *fruta con chile*, sold from a *carreta* or mobile *frutería* — is a cup or bag of freshly cut fruit, finished to order.

**Core fruit (year-round in a US Sun Belt city):**

- Watermelon
- Pineapple
- Mango
- Cucumber
- Jícama

**Seasonal / optional:** papaya, cantaloupe, honeydew, coconut, orange, strawberry

**Finish (this is the product):**

1. Lime juice, squeezed on the cup
2. Chile-lime salt (Tajín or equivalent)
3. Optional chamoy
4. Optional extra salt or chile piquín

Fruit is cut into **long spears** that stand in a clear cup, not grocery-store cubes. The look is the brand. Cubes make this a Del Monte cup with extra steps.

The combination is Mesoamerican in origin (fruit with chile and salt appears in Sahagún). The modern cart, Tajín, and chamoy are twentieth-century street food. It is one of the few street snacks that is actually fruit.

---

## 2. Honest finding: it already exists here

These carts are **not absent from the United States**. They are concentrated:

- Los Angeles and the San Gabriel Valley (Poblano *fruteros* are a documented labor system)
- Other California cities under the Compact Mobile Food Operation rules (2023)
- Texas, Arizona, and other Sun Belt cities with large Mexican populations

What *is* missing in most of the country:

| Exists today | Gap |
| --- | --- |
| Informal / semi-formal carts in Latino neighborhoods | Branded, permitted carts on gyms, campuses, beaches, office lunch corridors |
| Grocery fruit cups (cubes, 5–7 day hold, no chile-lime) | Cut-to-finish cups with lime / Tajín / chamoy |
| Viral knife skill (LA “fruit ninja” vendors on TikTok) | A de-skilled prep system so a second operator can run a second cart |
| Cheap cups competing on price ($8–$12 in LA, thin net) | Premium locations that can hold $10–$12 without a race to the bottom |

Rocío Rosales’s ethnography *Fruteros* (UC Press, 2020) is the best picture of the LA model: long days, paisano networks, cart debt, health and police pressure, and little upward mobility. LA Public Press (May 2025) reported legal fruit vendors spending about **$3,000/month per cart** and clearing **$50–$100/day** after tariffs and produce spikes. That is a livelihood, not a scalable company.

**Do not enter that market on price.** Enter a different location mix with a cleaner, faster, legal operation.

Mintel’s 2026 US fruit report is the demand-side confirmation: fruit is a **$66.2 billion** category with near-universal purchase intent, blocked by **cost, spoilage, and prep**. A cart that removes prep and serves it cold, in the street, is solving that friction.

## 3. The product

### 3.1 Menu (launch)

Keep the menu short. Complexity kills a cart.

| Item | Size | Price | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mixed fruit cup | 12 oz | $8 | Watermelon, pineapple, cucumber, jícama |
| Mixed fruit cup | 16 oz | $10 | Adds mango |
| Mixed fruit cup | 20 oz | $12 | Extra mango or pineapple |
| Mango cup | 16 oz | $11 | Highest labor; only if mango is cheap that week |
| Cucumber + jícama | 16 oz | $8 | Lowest COGS, heat-day seller |
| Add chamoy | — | $1 | Optional |
| Extra Tajín / lime | — | $0 | Always included |
| Agua fresca (phase 2) | 16 oz | $5 | Only after the fruit cup is proven |
| Coconut (phase 2) | — | $8 | Hardest cut; skip at launch |

No smoothies, no acai, no yogurt. Those need blenders, dairy TCS rules, and a different cart. Stay a fruit cart.

### 3.2 Non-negotiables

- Fruit is washed, then cut the same day. Leftover **cut melon is discarded** at close. Do not hold cut melon overnight.
- Cold hold at **41°F or below**. Cut melon is a Time/Temperature Control for Safety (TCS) food under the FDA Food Code.
- Spears, not cubes.
- Lime is fresh-squeezed, not bottled.
- Whole fruit on display is part of the sell. The cart should look like a Mexican *frutería*, not a concession stand.

### 3.3 Why grocery fruit cups are not the competitor

Packaged fruit cups (Del Monte, Ready Pac, and private label) are a **~$2.8 billion** US category (MarkWide, 2026). They win on shelf life and distribution. They lose on:

- Flavor (no lime, no chile, often underripe melon)
- Texture (cut days earlier)
- Theater (none)
- Price per ounce that looks cheap until you taste it

Compete on **fresh + finish + place**, not on being another cup in a cooler.

## 4. Operating model

Three ways to run this. Only one is the launch recommendation.

### A. Cut everything to order on the cart

Authentic. Slow. Highest permit burden (open food + TCS + warewashing). Needs an expert cutter. This is the Mexican / LA model. **Do not launch this way** unless the operator already has that knife speed.

### B. Commissary-cut, cart-sell sealed cups

Fast and easier to permit in some cities (prepackaged TCS). Kills the theater. Tastes closer to grocery. **Reject for the brand.**

### C. Hybrid (launch this)

Morning at a **commissary / central preparation facility**:

1. Buy produce at the terminal market (same day or previous afternoon)
2. Wash whole fruit
3. Core/peel pineapple and watermelon with NSF lever tools
4. Stage mango, cucumber, jícama whole or peeled
5. Ice and load the cart. Cut melon that is already opened stays at ≤41°F

On the cart:

1. Final spear cut with jigs (visible, 20–40 seconds)
2. Pack the cup
3. Lime, chile-lime, chamoy to order
4. Take payment

This keeps the show, drops the skill bar, and puts the messy wash/core work where health departments expect it.

### Daily rhythm (owner-operated, one cart)

| Time | Work |
| --- | --- |
| 5:30–7:00 | Terminal market or restaurant depot. Buy for the day only. |
| 7:00–9:00 | Commissary: wash, core, stage, ice, fill water tanks, log temps |
| 9:30–2:30 | Lunch corridor (campus, office, hospital, gym cluster) |
| 3:30–7:00 | Afternoon heat shift (park, beach, transit, event) *or* one strong lunch only |
| 7:30–8:30 | Commissary: dump wastewater, wash cart, discard leftover cut melon, store whole fruit |

Two shifts are possible. One excellent lunch beat is enough to validate.

### Sourcing

Buy at a **wholesale produce terminal**, not Costco, once volume is past a test weekend.

- San Antonio (cart one): Produce Terminal at 1500 S Zarzamora; SAWPM at 9342 SE Loop 410
- Houston (cart two): Houston Produce Center, Produce Row
- California study trip: LA Wholesale Produce Market
- Florida: Miami terminal

USDA AMS terminal prices (mid-August 2026, example markets):

- Mexican Kent mangoes: **$11–$14 per 4 kg flat**
- Costa Rica / Mexico pineapples: **$18–$22 per carton** (typically 6–8 count)
- Seedless watermelon: about **$5–$6 each**, or **$25 per carton** of 4s/5s
- Mexican limes: about **$35–$36 per 40 lb carton**

Seasonality is the P&L. When mango spikes, drop the mango cup and push cucumber/jícama/watermelon. Print a small “today’s fruit” slate. Never freeze a menu that ignores the market.

## 5. The machine (summary)

Full spec: [machine-spec.md](machine-spec.md)

**Do not wait for a custom invention to start.** Most of the cut already has an NSF, lever-action commercial tool. The remaining hard problem is **mango** (irregular oval, large pit) and a **cart-safe spear jig** that outputs standing sticks, not slices.

| Fruit | Launch tool | Invent later? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pineapple | Nemco Easy Pineapple Corer/Peeler (~$1,000, NSF, manual) | No |
| Watermelon | Commercial wedger / stick cutter | Only if volume is huge |
| Cucumber / jícama | French-fry / stick cutter | No |
| Mango | Sharp knife + cheap splitter until volume hurts | **Yes — this is the gap** |
| Coconut | Skip at launch | Maybe never; injury and time |

A factory slicer from Alibaba that outputs thin chips or cubes is the wrong machine. It would make this look like a supermarket cup and would not pass most cart plan reviews (electric, not integral, not NSF for US retail).

The moat, if there is one, is a **manual, NSF-path, multi-fruit spear station** that a second employee can learn in a day. Build that after one cart is profitable, not before.

## 6. Legal and food safety

Rules are **local**. This section is the pattern, not a permit. Hire a local health consultant for plan review before buying a cart.

### 6.1 Food-code facts that do not change by city

- **Cut melon is TCS.** Hold at **≤41°F**. FDA Food Code lists cut melons with cut leafy greens and cut tomatoes.
- Cut mango, pineapple, cucumber, and jícama are usually lower risk (lower pH), but mixed cups that include melon are treated as TCS. Run the whole cup as TCS.
- There is **no cook step**. Wash, prevent cross-contamination, keep it cold. FDA’s fresh-cut produce guidance exists because cut fruit has caused *Salmonella* and *Listeria* outbreaks (cantaloupe 2011; cantaloupe 2023 recall cascade).
- Discard leftover cut TCS food at end of day unless the local code allows a documented time/temperature hold. Default: **throw it away**.
- Equipment that touches food should be **NSF** (or equivalent sanitation-certified), commercial, not residential.
- Food handler cards for anyone who preps or serves. Certified food manager for the permit holder in most jurisdictions.

### 6.2 Texas (default first market) — as of 1 July 2026

House Bill 2844 moved mobile food vendor licensing to **Texas DSHS**. One state license replaces per-city health permits for the unit itself. Cities can still control **where** you park, fire inspection, and right-of-way.

Likely class for this cart: **Type II**. DSHS lists pushcarts, snow cones, hot dogs, and deli assembly in Type II (food prepared for immediate consumption, including cold holding). Type III is cooking on the unit. Confirm by phone before paying; cutting melon on the cart can still be argued up. Hybrid prep (core at the CPF, finish on the cart) is how you stay Type II.

| Item | 2026 DSHS fee (published) |
| --- | --- |
| Type II application | $618 |
| Type II pre-licensing inspection | $400 |
| Type III (full cook — avoid) | $876 + $500 inspection |

Typical unit requirements (Texas Food Establishment Rules pattern):

- Readily movable
- Mechanical refrigeration holding ≤41°F
- Hand sink with pressurized hot and cold water, soap, towels
- Three-compartment sink **or** a spare-utensil system if the local/state inspector allows it for limited prep
- Potable water tank; wastewater tank **≥15% larger**
- Commissary / central preparation facility agreement, visited each operating day
- Commercial equipment only

HB 2844 added a **commissary exemption checklist**. If the cart fails it, DSHS still requires a Central Preparation Facility. A house is illegal as a CPF. San Antonio Metro Health still cares where you dump tanks and store overnight. **Budget a commissary anyway** ($250–$500/month in SA) — the pineapple corer needs a stainless table even if the exemption later sticks. Details: [first-market.md](first-market.md).

Also needed: LLC or sole prop, EIN, sales tax permit, general liability (aim **$1M**), and whatever the **city** wants for parking. Private property (gym lot, brewery, campus, farmers market) is easier than sidewalk vending.

San Antonio extras: no vending from **residential** property; **300 feet from a school** during school hours; downtown needs a CCDO permit (skip it); parks need Parks permission; write restroom access into the host letter.

### 6.3 California (if you launch there instead)

Since 1 January 2023, sidewalk carts are **Compact Mobile Food Operations (CMFO)**. Cut fruit is **moderate-risk**. You need a permit, commissary, and — if you cut on the cart — an **integral handwashing sink** (pressurized, ≥5 gallons, 9×9×5 inches). Selling only prepackaged cups cut at the commissary can drop the on-cart sink requirement in LA County; confirm locally.

Whole uncut produce in ≤25 sq ft of display can be exempt. **Cut fruit is not exempt.**

California is the best place to **learn the craft** (watch *fruteros*) and a hard place to **win on price**. Use it as a study trip, not necessarily as cart one.

### 6.4 What will get you shut down

- Cooler full of cut watermelon with no thermometer
- Cutting on a folding table with no handwash
- Home kitchen as commissary
- Residential fridge on the cart
- Holding yesterday’s melon
- Parking on a public sidewalk with no vending permit

## 7. Where to put the cart

Heat + foot traffic + people who already pay $10 for a snack.

**Best first sites (private property with written permission):**

1. University lunch hour
2. Hospital / medical campus
3. Gym / CrossFit / rec-center cluster
4. Office park food-truck pad
5. Farmers market (weekend test)
6. Youth sports complex on Saturday
7. Beach / river / trailhead in season
8. Brewery / outdoor concert nights

**Avoid at launch:**

- Sidewalks that need a city vending lottery
- Directly next to an established *frutero* on their corner
- Indoor mall food courts (rent, exclusives)
- Neighborhoods where $10 fruit will be seen as a gringo markup on a $6 cup

The first 90 days are a **site test**, not a brand launch. One lunch beat that does 50+ cups is worth more than five weak spots.

## 8. Brand and positioning

Working name is not chosen. Do not freeze a name until the city is picked. Requirements:

- Readable in English and Spanish
- Does not pretend to be a taco truck
- Looks like fruit: color, umbrella, whole fruit on ice, clear cups

Positioning line:

> Fresh-cut Mexican street fruit. Lime, chile, chamoy. Cut in front of you.

Do **not** lead with “healthy.” Lead with cold, sweet-salty-spicy, and the visual of spears in a cup. Health is the excuse people use after they want it.

Social: the cut is the content. Film the jig, the lime, the Tajín dust. This is already proven (La Puente vendors went viral on cutting skill). Machine-assist should still look like a performance, not a factory.

## 9. Risks

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Produce price spike | Mango and pineapple move a lot | Daily menu; drop the expensive fruit that week |
| Heat / rain | Volume is weather | Indoor-adjacent sites; event calendar |
| Foodborne illness | One outbreak ends the brand | Commissary wash, 41°F, discard leftover cut melon, no home prep |
| Permit delay | Cart sits unpaid | Start as a commissary pop-up / farmers market |
| Knife injury | Classic cart injury | Jigs for pineapple/melon; cut-resistant gloves; skip coconut |
| Copycats | There is no secret recipe | Location leases and speed |
| Ethical | Scaling on the back of informal *fruteros* | Do not price-war their corners; hire fairly if you hire |

## 10. 90-day plan

Do not buy a $15k cart on day one. Prove cups, then buy the box.

### Days 1–14 — City and paper

- City is **San Antonio** unless produce or a site letter forces a change ([first-market.md](first-market.md))
- Form LLC, EIN, sales tax permit
- Food handler + food manager certs
- Walk Zarzamora terminal and SAWPM at 6 a.m.
- Eat at every fruit cart and *frutería* you can find. Time the queue. Note prices and fruit mix
- Call DSHS (512-406-2644) and San Antonio Metro Health **before** buying equipment. Ask: Type II vs III, CPF exemption vs commissary list, whether spare utensils replace a 3-comp sink, whether farmers-market booths are a different permit
- Get two commissary quotes from the Metro Health list

### Days 15–30 — Kitchen test, no cart

- Rent commissary kitchen hours only
- Buy a Nemco pineapple corer **or** skip it and use a $20 hand corer for the test (accept slower prep)
- Sell at **one** farmers market or one private-property pop-up with a written letter from the host
- Target: 40 cups in a day, twice. If you cannot hit 40 with friends-and-family traffic, the site is wrong, not the fruit
- Log every cup: fruit mix, cost, time to assemble, waste, weather

### Days 31–60 — Repeatable lunch

- Lock one weekday lunch site on private property
- Buy the NSF pineapple corer and a stick cutter if pineapple/jícama are slowing you
- Get the mobile unit **plan review** started (longest pole)
- Square / cash / tap. Track COGS weekly against [unit-economics.md](unit-economics.md)
- Kill any menu item that is slow or ugly

### Days 61–90 — The actual cart

- Take delivery of a used or new permitted-spec cart (fridge, hand sink, water tanks)
- Pass pre-licensing inspection
- Run 20 operating days. Goal: **50 cups/day average** at $10, COGS ≤ $3.50, leftover cut melon < 8% of fruit bought
- If that holds: keep the cart, add Saturday events, start a second site
- If it does not: you spent kitchen rent and a few tools, not a truck note

### After day 90

Only then: second cart, hired operator, custom mango jig, agua fresca, coconut.

## 11. Decision

The Mexican fruit cart is a real business in the US Sun Belt. It has not become a national chain because **skill, cold-chain, and permits** stop copy-paste expansion, and because the existing operators are optimized for survival on specific corners, not for gym-and-campus retail.

A working model for “make this work in the States” is:

1. **San Antonio first** (produce + cheap commissary; Houston later)
2. **Hybrid prep** (commissary wash/core + cart finish)
3. **NSF lever tools now**, mango jig later
4. **Private-property lunch sites** — gyms, hospitals, campuses, not sidewalks
5. **$10 cup, ~$3.20 COGS**, discard leftover melon
6. **Texas DSHS Type II** as the license; city rules for parking
7. **Pop-up before cart** so the expensive metal is earned

That is enough to start. The 90-day plan is the experiment. The machine is an accelerator, not the business.
