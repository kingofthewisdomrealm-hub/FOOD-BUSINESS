# Street fruit cart — working model

Bring the Mexican **fruta picada** cart to the United States as a legal, repeatable business: fresh-cut tropical fruit in a cup, finished with lime, chile-lime salt, and chamoy.

This repo is the research and operating plan, not a software product. First cart: **San Antonio**.

## The idea

In Mexico, carts selling cut mango, pineapple, watermelon, cucumber, and jícama are everywhere. In most of the United States they are not. Packaged grocery fruit cups exist, but they sit for days and usually skip the lime / Tajín / chamoy finish that makes the Mexican version a craving, not a chore.

The bottleneck is not demand. It is **prep skill, food-code compliance, and location**. Expert knife work is how the Mexican carts run; it is also why the model has not scaled into ordinary American commercial corridors. Existing NSF lever tools (pineapple corer, wedger, stick cutter) can de-skill most of the cut. A purpose-built mango/spear jig is the real equipment gap — not a factory processing line.

## Working assumptions (August 2026)

| Item | Assumption |
| --- | --- |
| First market | San Antonio (Houston = cart two, Austin = later high-price site) |
| Product | 16 oz mixed-fruit cup, cut fresh, lime + chile-lime + optional chamoy |
| Price | $10 baseline ($8 small / $12 large) |
| Cup COGS | about $3.20 |
| Gross margin | about 68% |
| Launch path | commissary pop-up first, permitted cart second |
| Capital to a legal cart | $15,000–$30,000 |

San Antonio wins cart one on produce (Zarzamora terminal + SAWPM on Loop 410) and commissary cost (~$250–$500/month). Texas DSHS Type II statewide licensing started **1 July 2026**; the city still controls where you park. Swap the city later; the cup and the math stay the same.

## What this is not

- A plan to undercut existing *fruteros* in Latino neighborhoods on price
- A packaged grocery fruit-cup brand
- A factory fruit-processing company
- A permission to skip health permits, refrigeration, or a commissary
